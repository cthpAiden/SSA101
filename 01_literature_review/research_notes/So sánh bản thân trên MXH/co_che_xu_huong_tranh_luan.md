## Key question 1: Theoretical base and named mechanisms (Festinger; Gibbons & Buunk INCOM; Wills downward; Collins upward)

### Takeaway
All four foundational sources verified with DOIs and exact pagination. Social comparison theory (Festinger 1954) is the root construct; INCOM (Gibbons & Buunk 1999) is the standard trait measure used in most social-media studies; Wills (1981) and Collins (1996) supply the downward/upward distinction that structures the social-media literature (contrast vs assimilation).

### Inferences
- The experimental meta-analytic effect of upward comparison on social media (g ≈ −0.2 to −0.3) is roughly an order of magnitude larger than the correlational "screen time ↔ well-being" associations reported in the causality debate (β ≈ −0.01 to −0.04; see Question 4). This supports framing the course project around *comparison* (a specific mechanism) rather than *time spent*.
- Body image is the outcome most sensitive to upward comparison (g = −0.31), consistent with appearance-focused platforms (Instagram, TikTok) being the focus of the experimental literature.

## Key question 2: Positivity bias / "highlight reel" and underestimating others' negative experiences

### Takeaway
Four verified sources show (a) people hide negative emotions more than positive ones and therefore underestimate how common others' negative experiences are (Jordan et al. 2011), (b) heavier Facebook use predicts believing "others are happier and have better lives" (Chou & Edge 2012, N = 425 undergraduates), (c) positive emotional expression is judged more appropriate than negative expression on all four major platforms, most strongly on Instagram (Waterloo et al. 2018, N = 1,201 aged 15–25), and (d) authenticity online predicts higher well-being longitudinally (Reinecke & Trepte 2014, N = 374).

### Cited Findings
- Jordan, A. H., Monin, B., Dweck, C. S., Lovett, B. J., John, O. P., & Gross, J. J. (2011). Misery has more company than people think: Underestimating the prevalence of others' negative emotions. *Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin, 37*(1), 120–135. https://doi.org/10.1177/0146167210390822 — Verified (Crossref: 37(1), 120–135; online-first 2010, print 2011). Four studies: Study 1a, participants reported negative emotions were "more private or hidden" than positive ones; Study 1b, participants "underestimated the peer prevalence of common negative, but not positive, experiences"; Study 2, underestimation of negative and overestimation of positive emotions "even for well-known peers," partially mediated by peers' suppression of negative emotions; Study 3, lower estimates of others' negative emotions predicted "greater loneliness and rumination and lower life satisfaction." CR 121; S2 176. Per-study Ns: **unverified** (not in abstract). Note: this is a general (offline) psychology study, often used as the theoretical basis for the online positivity bias. — [Crossref abstract](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1177/0146167210390822)
- Chou, H.-T. G., & Edge, N. (2012). ### Inferences
- Chained mechanism the report can state: people suppress negatives offline (Jordan) → online norms penalize negative expression further, most on Instagram (Waterloo) → heavy viewers infer that others are happier (Chou & Edge) → upward comparison produces contrast effects (McComb; Question 1).
- Instagram is simultaneously the platform where negative expression is least acceptable and positive expression highly acceptable (Waterloo), which matches its prominence in the body-image experiments.

## Key question 3: FOMO — scale, sample, correlations, and meta-analyses

### Takeaway
Przybylski et al. (2013) built the 10-item FoMO scale (N = 1,013), validated it on a nationally representative UK sample (N = 2,079) where FoMO correlated r = .40 with social-media engagement, and showed in 87 first-year students that FoMO relates to Facebook use in lectures and while driving. Meta-analyses (Fioravanti 2021, 33 samples, N = 21,473; Akbari 2021, 85 studies, N = 55,134) confirm medium-to-large pooled correlations (r ≈ .35–.49 for FoMO with platform use).

## Key question 4: The causality debate (Twenge; Orben & Przybylski; Haidt vs Odgers; Hancock; Ferguson; Vuorre; 2024–2026 meta-analyses)

### Takeaway
Both camps' headline numbers are verified. "Harm" side: Twenge et al. (2018; N = 506,820 adolescents) link post-2010 rises in depressive symptoms and suicide to new-media time; Twenge, Haidt et al. (2022) reanalysis finds median β = −0.11 to −0.24 for girls' social-media use. "Small/uncertain effect" side: Orben & Przybylski (2019; n = 355,358) find technology use explains at most 0.4% of well-being variance (YRBS median β = −0.035); Hancock et al. (2022; 226 papers, N = 275,728) find an overall r between −.02 and .04 (non-significant); Ferguson (2024/2025; k = 27 experiments) finds d = 0.088 [−0.018, 0.197]; Vuorre & Przybylski (2023; 72 countries, N = 946,798) find no evidence of widespread harm from Facebook adoption. Recent RCT meta-analyses (2025) find small but significant benefits of restriction (g ≈ 0.17–0.28), with intervention length as a key moderator.

- Exact pooled effect sizes for Ferguson et al. (2022), Cunningham et al. (2021) and Burnell et al. (2025) per outcome could not be read (publisher access blocked).

## Key question 5: Trends — is social comparison / appearance comparison rising among young adults? TikTok-era studies

### Takeaway
The only direct cohort comparison found is Locke (2025): two cohorts of undergraduates 27 years apart (N = 232 in 1997–98; N = 234 in 2024–25; 4,660 recorded comparisons). The 2020s cohort compared more with distant others (35% → >49%), more automatically (57% → 67%), more upward, felt less confident during comparisons (4.90 → 4.24 on a 7-point scale), and had lower self-esteem (M 3.51 → 2.79) and higher depression (BDI-II M 10.03 → 15.36). TikTok experiments (Pryde & Prichard 2022) show fitspiration videos raise state appearance comparison and negative mood in women 18–25.

### Cited Findings

- The rise in distant-target comparisons (35% → 49%) maps onto the "highlight reel" logic: distant others are judged mainly through curated online content (Chou & Edge's "friends not known personally" finding).

### Gaps
- No repeated cross-sectional or panel data specifically on *appearance* comparison orientation among 18–24-year-olds across years was found; Locke (2025) covers general social comparison, not appearance.
- 2024–2026 TikTok studies on university students found in the search (e.g., a Nigerian survey, N = 314; a Frontiers 2023 Chinese university sample) were in journals whose peer-review/indexing status could not be confirmed in this pass and are therefore omitted; treat as unverified leads.
- No study was found that isolates "age-milestone" or "success/achievement" content (e.g., graduation, job, marriage posts) as a comparison trigger with exact numbers; the closest verified material is Le Blanc-Brillon et al. (2025), which found no effect on *academic* self-esteem (Question 1). Mark this sub-topic as an evidence gap.

## Key question 6: Interventions that reduce comparison effects (limiting use, hiding likes, digital detox)

### Takeaway
Limiting use to ~30 min/day for 2–3 weeks (Hunt 2018, N = 143 undergraduates; Faulhaber 2023, N = 230 undergraduates) and a one-week full break (Lambert 2022, N = 154) produced significant reductions in depression, loneliness and anxiety; the hide-likes experiment (Wallace & Buil 2021, N = 280) shows hiding likes mainly dampens negative affect when likes are *high*, not when they are low. Meta-analytic benefits of restriction are small (g ≈ 0.17–0.28) and depend on duration (≥1 week).

### Inferences
- The most replicable young-adult intervention finding is "limit, don't abstain": ~30 min/day over 2–3 weeks (Hunt; Faulhaber) and the May et al. moderator analysis both favour limitation over full abstinence.
- Hiding likes addresses the *metric-comparison* channel, but Wallace & Buil's result implies it matters mainly for high-performing posts; it does not remove the "highlight reel" content channel.

### Gaps
- No field experiment on Instagram's real-world hide-likes rollout (2019–2021) with exact well-being outcomes was found in peer-reviewed form; Wallace & Buil (2021) is a scenario experiment.
- Effect sizes (d/η²) for Hunt et al. (2018), Faulhaber et al. (2023) and Tromholt (2016) could not be read from accessible abstracts.
